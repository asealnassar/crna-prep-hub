Vasopressors / Lesson 1 of 6 / Foundations

The Pressure Machine

Before a single drug makes sense, you need the machine it acts on: how pressure is actually generated, how smooth muscle actually contracts, and the three signalling pathways every vasopressor in this module runs through.

FOUNDATIONS

You already know norepinephrine raises blood pressure. That is not what this module is about.

In a CRNA interview, the follow-up is the question. Why does it raise blood pressure? Which receptor? What happens inside the cell? And why does the same drug do something different to the heart than it does to a blood vessel?

Every one of those answers runs through the material in this lesson. Get this right and the next five lessons are mostly bookkeeping — you will be able to derive a drug's effects instead of memorising them.

You crank SVR from 800 to 2000 in a patient with a failing ventricle. Does MAP rise by the amount the equation predicts?

No — and this is the single most useful idea in the lesson.

The equation is honest, but it hides a feedback loop. SVR is not just resistance the blood flows against; it is afterload, the pressure the ventricle has to generate before the aortic valve will open at all.

A healthy ventricle absorbs that. It has contractile reserve, so stroke volume barely moves and MAP climbs roughly as predicted. A failing ventricle does not. Raise its afterload and stroke volume falls, which drops CO, which claws back part of the MAP you just bought — sometimes most of it.

You end up with a higher number on the monitor and less blood actually moving. Lesson 3 turns this into a bedside scenario with phenylephrine.

02

The half of the circulation nobody watches

Ask most nurses what a vasopressor constricts and they will say arteries. Arteries are only half the story, and arguably the less interesting half.

Roughly 70% of your blood volume sits in the venous system at any moment. Veins are thin-walled and compliant — they are a reservoir, not a pipe. That reservoir splits into two pools:

  • Unstressed volume — blood that fills the veins without stretching them. It generates no pressure. It is parked.
  • Stressed volume — blood that actually distends the vessel wall. This is what generates mean systemic filling pressure, the upstream pressure that drives blood back to the right atrium.

Venoconstriction squeezes the reservoir and converts parked blood into pressurised blood. Nothing is added, but the effective circulating volume rises, venous return rises, preload rises, and by Frank–Starling, stroke volume rises.

Why this matters for drug selection

A pure arterial constrictor raises afterload and gives the ventricle nothing back. A drug that also constricts veins raises afterload and hands the ventricle extra preload to work with — partially paying for its own afterload cost. That difference is a large part of why norepinephrine behaves better than phenylephrine in a shocked patient, and it has nothing to do with which is the "stronger" pressor.

03

How a blood vessel actually squeezes

"α₁ causes vasoconstriction" is where nursing school stops. It is the beginning of the answer an interviewer wants, not the end.

Vascular smooth muscle has no troponin. The switch that turns contraction on is a different one entirely, and every Gq-linked vasopressor in this module — norepinephrine, phenylephrine, vasopressin, angiotensin II — converges on it.

Go deeper · Calcium sensitisation

The diagram implies contraction is purely a function of how much calcium arrives. It is not, quite. Gq-linked receptors also produce DAG, which activates protein kinase C — and PKC, along with the RhoA/Rho-kinase pathway, inhibits MLCP.

The consequence is elegant: the vessel can contract harder at the same calcium concentration, because the off-switch has been disabled. This is called calcium sensitisation, and it is why the IP₃ arm of Gq is only half of what these receptors do. If an interviewer asks what DAG contributes, this is the answer they are hoping for.

04

Three pathways, and only three

Every receptor in this module — α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, D₁, V₁ₖ, V₂, AT₁ — is a G-protein-coupled receptor, and each one couples to one of three G-proteins. Learn the three pathways and you have learned the mechanism of every drug in this module.

05

Why cAMP contracts the heart and relaxes the vessel

This is the concept that separates a good answer from an elite one, because on its face it looks like a contradiction. β₁ and β₂ both couple to Gs. Both raise cAMP. Both activate PKA. Yet β₁ stimulation makes the heart squeeze harder and β₂ stimulation makes vessels go slack.

The resolution: PKA is not an effect. It is an enzyme looking for substrates. What happens next depends on which proteins are sitting in that particular cell.

Same signal, different toolbox. The cardiac myocyte's PKA targets all amplify calcium; the smooth muscle cell's targets all suppress it or disable the kinase that uses it.

Say it this way in an interview

"cAMP isn't an instruction to contract or relax — it's an amplifier. PKA phosphorylates whatever substrates that cell type expresses. In the myocyte those substrates increase calcium availability, so you get inotropy and chronotropy. In vascular smooth muscle they inhibit myosin light chain kinase and lower cytosolic calcium, so you get relaxation. Same messenger, different downstream targets."

A drug is a pure β-agonist with roughly equal β₁ and β₂ activity. Predict what happens to MAP.

It may not move much — and it can fall.

β₁ raises CO through inotropy and chronotropy, which pushes MAP up. But β₂ simultaneously dilates the vasculature, dropping SVR. Return to MAP ≈ CO × SVR: you are raising one term while lowering the other, and the product can land anywhere.

This is the reason a β-agonist is an inotrope rather than a vasopressor. Hold onto it — it is exactly why epinephrine's hemodynamic profile is more complicated than "raises everything," which is Lesson 3.

06

The reflex that fights your drug

You give a pressor. Pressure rises. The body does not simply accept this — it has a fast negative-feedback loop whose entire job is to hold arterial pressure constant, and it will actively oppose you.

Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch fire faster as pressure rises. That traffic runs up the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves to the brainstem, and the response is two-part: parasympathetic outflow increases and sympathetic outflow decreases.

Separate the drug from the reflex

Norepinephrine has real β₁ activity, so on receptors alone you would predict tachycardia. Most patients do not become tachycardic — some slow down. That is not the drug failing to hit β₁. It is the direct β₁ effect and the baroreflex pulling in opposite directions, with the reflex often winning.

An interviewer asking "if norepinephrine hits β₁, why isn't everyone tachycardic?" is testing exactly one thing: whether you can distinguish a direct receptor effect from a compensatory reflex. Most candidates cannot.

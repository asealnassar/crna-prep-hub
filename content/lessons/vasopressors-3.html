Three Ways to Break the Pattern

Norepinephrine was the clean case. These three are where the template gets tested — one adds a receptor, one removes a receptor, and one refuses to behave predictably at all.

THREE DRUGS

You now have a working method: name the receptors, run each cascade separately, then combine them and check the reflex. Lesson 2 applied it to a drug that cooperates.

These three do not. Each one breaks the pattern in a different, instructive way — and two of them come wrapped in teaching so oversimplified that repeating it in an interview will cost you.

Epinephrine

α₁ · β₁ · β₂

Adds β₂. Now the drug has a vasodilator arm competing with its own vasoconstrictor arm.

Phenylephrine

α₁ only

Removes β₁. Shows you exactly what norepinephrine's β₁ activity was quietly paying for.

Dopamine

D₁ · β₁ · α₁ · indirect

Four mechanisms, one of which depends on the patient's own neurotransmitter stores.

01

Epinephrine: the drug that argues with itself

Epinephrine hits all three adrenergic receptors that matter here. Two of them do opposite things to the vasculature.

  • α₁ → Gq → vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR
  • β₁ → Gs → ↑ contractility and rate → ↑ CO
  • β₂ → Gs → vasodilation, particularly in skeletal muscle → ↓ SVR

Return to MAP ≈ CO × SVR. Epinephrine reliably raises CO. What it does to SVR depends on which of α₁ and β₂ is winning — and that balance shifts with concentration.

The oversimplification to avoid

You will have been taught "low dose is beta, high dose is alpha," with tidy numeric cutoffs. Directionally there is something real here — β receptors are engaged at lower concentrations than α₁ — but the tidy version is wrong in three ways worth being able to name.

The transition is gradual and overlapping, not stepped: β effects do not switch off when α arrives. Receptor density varies by vascular bed, so different circulations cross over at different points. And the plasma concentration produced by a given infusion rate varies enormously between patients — so a number that behaves one way in one patient behaves differently in the next.

At lower concentrations, epinephrine can raise cardiac output while the diastolic pressure falls. Why?

β₂ is dilating faster than α₁ is constricting.

β₁ raises stroke volume, which widens the pulse pressure and lifts the systolic. Meanwhile β₂-mediated vasodilation in skeletal muscle drops SVR, and diastolic pressure depends heavily on SVR — so the diastolic falls.

The result looks contradictory on the monitor: a rising systolic, a falling diastolic, a MAP that barely moves, and a genuinely improved cardiac output. Every part of that is predictable from the receptor list.

Why the lactate rises — and why that isn't necessarily bad news

Start epinephrine and the lactate often climbs. The reflex interpretation is that perfusion is worsening. Frequently it is not, and this is one of the most quotable mechanisms in the whole module.

How to say it under questioning

"A rising lactate on epinephrine isn't automatically a perfusion failure. β₂ stimulation raises cAMP, accelerates glycogenolysis and glycolysis, and stimulates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — so pyruvate production outruns mitochondrial capacity and the excess becomes lactate aerobically. I'd interpret the number alongside the trend in other perfusion markers rather than in isolation." Then stop — do not overclaim that the lactate never reflects hypoperfusion. In a shocked patient it can be both at once, and saying so is a strength.

02

Phenylephrine: the control experiment

Phenylephrine is essentially a selective α₁ agonist. It runs the Gq cascade from Lesson 1 and nothing else of consequence — which makes it the cleanest illustration in the module of what happens when you raise resistance and offer the ventricle nothing in return.

The trace is short: α₁ → Gq → PLCβ → IP₃/DAG → ↑Ca²⁺ → MLCK → contraction → ↑SVR → ↑MAP.

What follows is where it gets interesting. Two consequences arrive that the drug did not directly cause.

The idea the whole module is built around

A higher blood pressure does not mean better perfusion. Pressure is what you can measure easily; flow is what delivers oxygen. Phenylephrine can improve the first while reducing the second, and a patient can look better on the monitor while their tissues receive less.

Go deeper · So is phenylephrine a bad drug?

No — and an interviewer may well push here to see whether you over-corrected.

The falling-CO problem is worst when the ventricle is already struggling. In a patient with good ventricular function whose problem is genuinely vasodilation — a spinal or epidural-induced sympathectomy, say, or hypotension after an anaesthetic induction — a selective α₁ agonist addresses the actual defect, and a healthy ventricle absorbs the added afterload with little loss of stroke volume.

Phenylephrine also constricts veins, which recruits some preload back. The honest position is not "phenylephrine lowers cardiac output" but "phenylephrine offers no inotropic support, so whether output falls depends on how much afterload reserve that particular ventricle has."

03

Dopamine: four mechanisms and a myth

Dopamine is the least predictable drug in this module, and the reasons are worth understanding rather than memorising.

  • D₁ → Gs → ↑cAMP → vasodilation in renal, mesenteric and coronary beds.
  • β₁ → Gs → ↑ contractility and rate.
  • α₁ → Gq → vasoconstriction, engaged at higher concentrations.
  • Indirect — it also triggers norepinephrine release from sympathetic nerve terminals.

That last mechanism is the one that makes dopamine genuinely different. Part of its effect is not the drug at all — it is the patient's own stored norepinephrine being released.

Two claims to retire

"Renal-dose dopamine protects the kidneys." The D₁-mediated renal vasodilation and increased urine output are real. What has not held up is the inference that this prevents or treats acute kidney injury — more urine is not more renal recovery. Presenting the observation is fine; presenting the conclusion is not.

"Low dose is renal, mid is cardiac, high is pressor." Same three problems as with epinephrine, plus one worse: the plasma concentration achieved at any given infusion rate varies dramatically between patients, so the same number lands in different parts of the curve in different people. The ranges describe a tendency, not a switchboard.

Two patients get identical dopamine infusions. One responds briskly; the other barely moves. Beyond dose-response variability, what could explain it?

Their norepinephrine stores differ.

A meaningful part of dopamine's effect is indirect — it releases norepinephrine from sympathetic nerve terminals. That mechanism needs something in the terminals to release.

In chronic heart failure, or after prolonged shock with sustained sympathetic activation, those stores can be substantially depleted. The indirect component then contributes far less, and the patient responds mainly to dopamine's direct receptor effects. A drug whose effect partly depends on the patient's own reserves is inherently harder to titrate than one that acts entirely on its own.

Dopamine is also more arrhythmogenic than norepinephrine at comparable pressure targets — unsurprising given the β₁ stimulation plus the additional endogenous norepinephrine it liberates. That combination of unpredictable dose-response and higher arrhythmia burden is why it has largely been displaced as a first-line vasopressor.

The reasoning move to practise

Never answer "what does this drug do to heart rate" from memory. Answer it in three beats: what the receptors do directly, what the pressure change provokes reflexively, and which of the two is likely to win in this patient. That structure works for every drug in the module, including the two you meet next.

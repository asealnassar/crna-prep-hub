Vasopressors/ Lesson 5 of 6/ Integration

Predicting Instead of Remembering

Six drugs, one method. This lesson is not a chart to memorise — it is the four-step reasoning you run when someone names a drug you have not thought about in months.

INTEGRATION

Memorised charts fail in interviews for a specific reason: the follow-up question is never on the chart.

You can memorise that phenylephrine causes bradycardia. Then someone asks why it doesn't in a heart-transplant patient, and the chart has nothing to say. Derivation survives that question because it regenerates the answer from the receptor rather than retrieving it from memory.

So this lesson gives you the method first, drills it, and only then shows the table — as somewhere to check your work, not somewhere to learn from.

01

The four steps

01

Name the receptors

Which of α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, D₁, V₁ₖ and AT₁ does this drug engage, and roughly in what proportion? Everything downstream depends on getting this right, and it is the only part that is genuinely memorisation.

02

Run each cascade separately

Gq constricts vessels. Gs in the heart raises rate and force. Gs in vascular smooth muscle relaxes. Gi damps. Do not combine yet — mixing at this stage is how candidates end up with a muddle they cannot defend.

03

Combine, and look for opposition

Now sum them. The interesting question is whether any two arms pull against each other — β₂ dilating while α₁ constricts, or rising afterload eroding the stroke volume that β₁ just improved.

04

Apply the reflex, then ask about the patient

Did MAP move enough to load the baroreceptors? If so the reflex pushes back on rate and tone. Then the question that separates good answers from elite ones: can this particular patient mount that reflex, and does this particular ventricle have afterload reserve?

Why step 4 has two halves

Steps 1–3 give you the drug's behaviour in an average patient. Step 4 is where you make it about the patient in front of you — and that is almost always where the follow-up question lives. "It usually causes bradycardia" invites a challenge. "It usually causes bradycardia, unless the reflex arc is impaired — a denervated heart, autonomic neuropathy, deep sedation" pre-empts it.

02

Worked once, slowly

Take phenylephrine and run all four steps out loud.

Full derivation · phenylephrine

Step 1. Selective α₁. Nothing else of consequence.

Step 2. α₁ → Gq → PLCβ → IP₃ and DAG → calcium up, MLCP inhibited → MLCK phosphorylates myosin light chain → smooth muscle contracts. Arterially that is ↑SVR; venously it is ↑preload.

Step 3. Only one arm, so nothing opposes it directly — but ↑SVR is ↑afterload, and afterload erodes stroke volume. There is no β₁ here to defend against that.

Step 4. SVR up means MAP up, which loads baroreceptors, which raises vagal tone and slows the SA node. So HR falls and SV falls, and CO = HR × SV falls on both terms — in a patient who can mount the reflex and whose ventricle lacks afterload reserve. In a healthy ventricle with brisk reflexes, the bradycardia still appears but CO holds up far better.

Notice that the final sentence is where the marks are. Anyone can say "phenylephrine causes reflex bradycardia." Very few add the conditions under which it does and doesn't.

Read the columns, not the rows

Scan the Inotropy column. Three drugs have a dash — phenylephrine, vasopressin, angiotensin II — and all three carry the same limitation, because it is the same limitation. Any pure vasoconstrictor raises afterload and offers the ventricle nothing back. Once you see that, three separate facts collapse into one idea, and you stop needing to remember them individually.

05

Four traps

Each of these is a place candidates reliably lose ground.

Confusing the direct effect with the reflex. "Norepinephrine causes bradycardia" is wrong as stated — β₁ stimulation is chronotropic. The reflex opposes it, and sometimes wins. Always name which force you are describing.

Treating pressure as perfusion. MAP is the number you can see; flow is what delivers oxygen. Phenylephrine can improve one while reducing the other.

Believing tidy dose cutoffs. Both epinephrine and dopamine are taught with numeric thresholds that do not exist. Occupancy is continuous, receptor density differs by bed, and plasma levels vary between patients.

Overclaiming. Cardiac output is usually preserved on norepinephrine. Epinephrine-associated lactate is often aerobic. In a shocked patient it can be both at once. Saying so is a strength, not a hedge — certainty where the evidence is softer is exactly what a good examiner probes.

Vasopressors/ Lesson 4 of 6/ Vasopressin · Angiotensin II

When Catecholamines Stop Answering

Every drug so far has worked through adrenergic receptors. This lesson is about what you reach for when those receptors have stopped listening — and why a completely different pathway is the point, not a detail.

NON-ADRENERGIC

A patient has been in vasodilatory shock for eighteen hours. Norepinephrine is running high, epinephrine has been added, and the MAP is still 54. Each escalation buys less than the one before.

The instinct is to push harder. The better question is why the pathway has stopped responding — because once you can answer that, the reason a non-catecholamine works becomes obvious rather than arbitrary.

01

Why the adrenergic pathway runs out

Three things happen to adrenergic signalling during prolonged, severe vasodilatory shock, and none of them are fixed by more drug.

  • Receptor desensitisation and downregulation. Sustained high catecholamine exposure uncouples receptors from their G-proteins and reduces surface receptor density. The ligand still arrives; fewer functional receptors are there to receive it.
  • Acidosis. Adrenergic responsiveness falls as pH drops — and the shocked patient is usually acidotic, so the impairment compounds exactly when you need the drug most.
  • Vasoplegic machinery. Inflammatory mediators drive nitric oxide production and open ATP-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle. Hyperpolarised cells resist contraction regardless of which receptor you stimulate.

So escalating the same drug means pushing harder on a mechanism that has degraded. A drug acting through an entirely separate receptor is not just an alternative — it is the response the physiology actually calls for.

The frame to carry through this lesson

The body maintains vascular tone through three independent systems: the adrenergic system, vasopressin, and the renin–angiotensin system. Each has its own receptor, and two of the three have nothing to do with catecholamines. Modern practice increasingly recruits more than one at moderate doses instead of driving a single one to its ceiling.

02

Vasopressin has two jobs, and they are not related

Vasopressin is a nonapeptide from the posterior pituitary — not a catecholamine, not structurally related to one. It acts on two receptors that do completely different things in completely different tissues. Keeping them separate is the single most common failure point when candidates describe this drug.

The vascular arm · V₁ₖ

Notice the cascade is identical to α₁ from Lesson 2 — Gq, PLC, IP₃ and DAG, calcium, MLCK. Different receptor, different ligand, same machinery.

That shared ending is exactly why it works when adrenergic receptors have failed: the contractile apparatus was never the problem, only the door you were knocking on.

The renal arm · V₂

PKA drives vesicles carrying aquaporin-2 into the apical membrane of collecting duct cells, making the tubule permeable to water. Water follows the medullary gradient, and urine concentrates.

This is the arm that has nothing to do with blood pressure — and the reason vasopressin and ADH are the same molecule under two names.

Go deeper · The vasoplegia-specific effects

Beyond straightforward V₁ₖ constriction, vasopressin acts on two of the mechanisms that make vasoplegic shock resistant in the first place. It inhibits the ATP-sensitive potassium channels that hyperpolarise vascular smooth muscle, and it attenuates nitric-oxide-mediated cGMP signalling.

So it is not simply a substitute constrictor. It works partly by interfering with the machinery that was defeating your catecholamines. That is a genuinely strong point to make if an interviewer asks why it is effective specifically in vasoplegia rather than in shock generally.

03

The deficiency argument

There is a second reason vasopressin suits prolonged vasodilatory shock, and it is about the patient rather than the drug.

Early in shock, endogenous vasopressin release rises sharply — an appropriate response to hypotension. Sustained over hours, that release depletes pituitary stores, and plasma levels fall to values that are inappropriately low for the degree of hypotension present. This is often described as a relative vasopressin deficiency.

That reframes what you are doing: at the low fixed doses used in shock, you are less "adding a vasopressor" than restoring a hormone the patient has run out of. It also explains why vasopressin is typically given at a fixed low dose rather than titrated the way a catecholamine is — the goal is replacement, not escalation.

Where to stop

The depletion observation is well described. Be careful not to extend it into a claim that measuring a vasopressin level should guide therapy, or that every vasodilatory shock patient is deficient. Present the mechanism as a rationale, not a diagnostic test — and if an examiner pushes on how well-established it is, the honest answer is that the physiology is clearer than the outcome data.

04

What it costs

Two limitations follow directly from the receptor list, and you should be able to derive both.

No inotropy. There is no V-receptor on the cardiac myocyte doing what β₁ does. Vasopressin is a pure vasoconstrictor, which puts it in the same category as phenylephrine from Lesson 3: it raises afterload and offers the ventricle nothing in return. In a failing heart, cardiac output can fall.

Regional vasoconstriction is not uniform. V₁ₖ density varies by vascular bed. Skin, skeletal muscle and splanchnic circulations constrict readily; at higher doses this shows up as digital and mesenteric ischaemia. Coronary vasoconstriction is also possible. This is the main reason vasopressin is used at low fixed doses rather than pushed upward — the dose-limiting problem is regional ischaemia, not systemic pressure.

05

Angiotensin II and the third system

The renin–angiotensin system is the third independent lever on vascular tone. Knowing where angiotensin II comes from matters as much as knowing what it does.

Like vasopressin, angiotensin II offers no inotropy, and its value in refractory vasoplegia is the same argument: a third receptor, independent of the two you have already saturated.

06

Three doors, one room

α₁, V₁ₖ and AT₁ are different receptors responding to different ligands — and all three couple to Gq and end at the same MLCK switch. Saturating one says nothing about the availability of the other two.

Adding a second pathway at a modest level often achieves more than driving the first one higher. Why, mechanistically?

Because each receptor pool saturates independently.

Occupancy follows a saturating curve. Once most α₁ receptors are engaged — and, in prolonged shock, once a fraction of them have been downregulated or uncoupled — additional catecholamine adds progressively less constriction while continuing to add its dose-related costs: chronotropy, arrhythmogenicity, myocardial oxygen demand.

V₁ₖ and AT₁ are separate receptor pools, unaffected by adrenergic saturation, and both feed the same Gq cascade. Recruiting them starts on the steep part of a fresh curve rather than the flat part of an exhausted one.

This is the physiological argument behind using more than one pathway at moderate doses instead of maximising a single one.
